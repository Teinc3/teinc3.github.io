## Why FDE matters
*Last updated: 26/10/2026*
*Genre: Society*

### Death of the SWE ladder
It should be quite obvious as to why SWE is "dying". Well, strictly speaking,
we're talking only about Junior SWE positions. The rationale is that in a world
dominated by ever increasing agent orchestration abilities, requiring young
graduates to implement boilerplate code is no longer functionally necessary nor
cost beneficial to the company when an AI agent can do it in a few weeks under
suitable guidance from promopting, at a fraction the cost compared to
hiring proper young engineers (if we exclude cutting costs by hiring people from
low income countries remotely + proper prompting, not your average
"make no mistakes" prompt). That guidance is exactly the skill needed for
mid-level and even senior engineers because you still need a direction for how
the project will grow or evolve, what scope to include/cut, and how it fits
business needs (usually abstracted by product managers/solution engineers). None
of these are usually delegated to the junior level whose sole purpose is to
churn out PRs for review until they are trusted sufficiently by management to
make those executive decisions themselves.

So when you cut the traditional corporate ladder (or make it significantly
exclusive only to retain talent for reliable mid-senior engineers in few years
time) you end up exactly with the current SWE job market: sustainable (or even
strong) growth in the mid-senior levels of engineers, yet junior openings
slumping down to 80% of its peak during ChatGPT 3.0's release. And with the
access to a significantly increased talent pool of people who actaully know how
to steer an agent (despite having 0 fundamentals in traditional SWE), it only
makes sense that demand goes down, supply goes up. Your competition gets
too heated, and now CS/CompEng grads are in a job crisis.

### FDE Skills Floor
Now contrast this to an FDE, required to do all the following at once:
- Scope project requirements to relate to business requirements under limited
    time, funding and resources (Skills of a Product Manager)
- Come up with a realistic and feasible technical specification/stack (Skills of
    a mid-senior engineer)
- Implement a demonstrable prototype in a few days (Skills of a vibecoder)
- Ensure code quality/fidelity, making sure code is "algorithmically correct"
    and all errors are handled (Skills of a junior engineer)
- Communicate clearly and be able to convince BOTH internal colleagues and
    external partners why and how the technical solution you chose/proposed
    solves the exact business problem they have (Skills of a Retailer)

And we're not talking about personality requirements:
- The ability to maintain performance output during long, gruesome travel hours
- The ability to switch between people-facing charisma and technical fidelity
- The operational context to relate technical problems to business requirements

Do you know how absolutely brutal the amount of skills required for just this
one single position is? Maybe 1% of all junior SWEs, 5-10% with targetted 
training. Obviously this leads to the entrance bar being extremely high,
but as long as you train all of these skills to a reasonable degree of
proficiency, you're getting in, because the increase in supply is buffered by
the skill floor and can never blow past the growth in demand in the short term.
(ref. minimum wage laws)

### Job Growth and Security
And it's not corporate demand too. Sorry for not being able to quote a source
here but statistics wise FDEs are around 3x more likely to become a successful
entrepreneur out of any other job type. Simply because all of these skills 
highly overlap with those that a founder needs.

If you stay in FDE, you get good pay and good skill growth. If you then transfer
these skills over to building your company - you know exactly what investors
need, how do you bargain with them, how do you hire engineers and other talents.
You also get to know your potential clients a lot better, having previously
worked with them during your FDE work. But still, probability is harsh, and 
let's suppose it fails you and you're broke and bankrupt now. No problem,
because you already have experience with the FDE model you're almost certainly
getting a job in the same domain back, if not in a better position than when you
resigned initially to build your own project, simply because FDEs are still
heavily undersupplied and will always be. And say you don't want to be an FDE
anymore - but look! demand for mid-senior SWEs, PMs, architects, management or
even consultancy roles has only risen. Your existing background knowledge can
literally faciliate transition to them, provided you re-train the required
specialisations.
